How it works · in plain English

A weather station for the stock market

Every weekday, this site checks a handful of public market and economy readings and asks one question: are the conditions that came before past crashes showing up right now?

It doesn't predict the future. Like a weather station, it tells you what the conditions are and how often weather like this turned stormy in the past.

Six steps, every weekday

From raw public data to the reading on the dashboard, refreshed each morning and evening
  1. Step 1

    Collect the readings

    A script runs on a schedule and pulls in public data: the Federal Reserve's FRED database, the U.S. Treasury, CBOE, the CFTC, FINRA, SEC filings and a few more. It's the same kind of data economists and fund managers look at.

    No guessing at this step. The numbers are stored exactly as published.

  2. Step 2

    Give each one a traffic light

    Each core reading gets a colour: green (calm), amber (worth watching) or red (stressed). The cut-off points are written down in advance in a rulebook, and the same number always gets the same colour.

    No AI decides the colours. It's plain arithmetic, so anyone can check it.

  3. Step 3

    Wait to be sure

    Markets have bad days all the time. One red day doesn't count. A reading has to stay red for a few days in a row before it's "confirmed."

    It's like a smoke alarm that ignores burnt toast but still goes off for a real fire.

  4. Step 4

    Count the red lights

    One stressed reading by itself is common. The real warning sign in past crashes was several of them going red together: fear jumps, borrowing gets more expensive, stocks fall and jobs weaken all at once.

    So the site counts confirmed reds. Once enough are red together, the overall stress light comes on.

  5. Step 5

    Check how it went last time

    Next to the traffic lights is a simple drawdown risk band. It combines two things: how far stocks have already fallen from their peak, and how jumpy the market is (the VIX "fear gauge").

    Each band comes with a plain record: out of all the past days that looked like today, how often a big fall followed. The band's cut-off points were fixed before any live results came in, so they can't be quietly retuned to look good afterward.

    An earlier, fancier model was retired after it failed a fair test against this simple one. Being honest about that matters more than looking clever.

  6. Step 6

    Let AI explain, but on a short leash

    An AI reads the day's data and writes the short story you see on the dashboard: what changed and why it might matter.

    It doesn't get to make up crash odds. It starts from the historical odds for today's band and can only nudge them one small, fixed step up or down, giving its reasons. Each of those calls is later scored against what actually happened.

The six core readings

The ones that get traffic lights. The dashboard shows today's values, colours and history.

Fear gauge (VIX)

How big a swing traders expect in the S&P 500 over the next month. It spikes when people panic.

Junk-bond spread

The extra interest riskier companies have to pay to borrow. When lenders get nervous, it widens.

Stock market fall

How far the S&P 500 is below its all-time high. Every crash starts as a small dip.

10-year Treasury yield

What the U.S. government pays to borrow for ten years. If it gets too high, mortgages and loans cost more for everyone.

Sahm rule

Watches whether unemployment is rising quickly. Historically it has been a reliable early sign of a recession.

Fed policy

Whether the Federal Reserve is holding interest rates or starting to cut. A sudden rush to cut often means the Fed sees trouble.

Around these six, the dashboard also shows context that doesn't get a traffic light: how big investors are positioned, what large funds are buying in their SEC filings, the shape of the yield curve and a checklist on government debt. It also has a page comparing well-known economists' public views with the live data.

What this is, and what it isn't

It is

  • A daily, rules-based snapshot of market stress.
  • Built only from public data, with the rules written down ahead of time.
  • Honest about its track record, including the parts that didn't work.

It isn't

  • Investment advice or a recommendation to buy or sell anything.
  • A crystal ball. Calm readings don't promise calm markets.
  • Run by a company. It's one person's personal project.
